## 2. Recurring themes, ranked
1. **EPOS and sales history only describe the past.** Every founder and lead says this: range reviews reward last year, and nothing covers what was never ranged, priced or bought.
2. **Cold start for challengers.** A brand with no sales history can't win a listing. Buyers only want proof of incrementality, and DTC or social traction doesn't transfer to their shelf.
3. **The buyer owns the risk but gets biased evidence.** Supplier decks are built to win the meeting; data partners describe a shelf that has already traded.
4. **Instinct fills the gaps.** Decisions are argued, then shipped untested, and the most confident voice wins.
5. **Say and do don't match.** Shoppers say they want healthy or gut-friendly food but don't buy it. Star ratings and paid UGC are untrustworthy, and they care about why someone nearly bought something.
6. **Trends move faster than retail systems.** They want to separate genuine momentum from noise.
7. **Traceability and confidence.** "A number that cannot be traced back to its source is worse than no number at all" (careers page). They are allergic to black boxes.
8. **Generic LLMs lack retail context.** Being "just a GPT wrapper" is the insult.
9. **Each retailer is different.** Shopper archetypes and missions differ between Tesco and Waitrose, and by store or region.

## 4. Hit-a-nerve implications

**Five hack angles:**
1. **"Incrementality Defence" for a challenger with no EPOS.** Given a new SKU and a target retailer's range, predict where its volume comes from: new shoppers, a switch from a named SKU, or cannibalisation. Output a one-page, buyer-ready case with every number traceable to its source. This hits Adam's chicken-and-egg problem, David's "what happens if I say yes" and Gianni's "digestible chunks".
2. **Ghost-delist / dead-whitespace detector.** For each empty cell in a category matrix, work out whether it is empty because nothing has been tried or because products were tried and failed. Use delisting history, trend decay and say/do signals. This makes Sean's thesis operational, and they haven't shipped it.
3. **Say/do gap or near-miss analyser.** Mine the "what I nearly bought and why I didn't" data (voice memos, reviews) to find rejection drivers such as trust, cost or effort, and output one change to make (price, pack, claim, placement). Fits Track 1, Camille's "trust, cost, can-I-be-bothered" and the "won the shelf but not the trolley" line.
4. **Synthetic-shopper grounding auditor.** A calibration harness that scores how well synthetic persona answers match held-out real behaviour, gives a confidence score per segment, and flags when the twins are hallucinating. It speaks directly to Ege's grounding point and the AI Data Engineer evaluation remit. It complements their Digital Twins rather than copying them.
5. **Retailer-specific pitch re-mapper.** Show the same product reframed for Tesco, Waitrose and Ocado by archetype and mission, with what changes and why. Adam's "don't use the same deck" pain, plus the Ocado tie-in. A possible extra: AI shopping agents as a new kind of shopper that EPOS can't see.

**Execution tips:** confidence scores and source links on every number, a concrete user moment (Oriol), and a clean demo story (Gianni).

**Three things to avoid:**
1. **A generic "ask your retail data" chatbot, trend-buzz scorer, or review summariser.** They already have the Query Agent, Breakthrough and Buzz scores, Culture & Trend Signals, and plain-English review search.
2. **A synthetic-persona panel or "AI buyer that critiques your pitch".** The Buyer Agent and Signal Twins shipped in April. Only go here if you add grounding or validation they lack.
3. **Anything driven only by sales history or star ratings, or unsourced LLM numbers.** That repeats the exact thing they rant about, and "GPT wrapper" is Adam's insult.
